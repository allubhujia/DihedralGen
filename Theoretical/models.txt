gnn.py (MolecularGNN) takes your peptide molecule represented as a graph — atoms are nodes with features (like atom type, charge, coordinates), and bonds are edges — and runs message passing.
Each GCNConv layer lets every atom "talk" to its neighbors, aggregating information about its local chemical environment. After 3 layers, each atom's representation encodes not just its own features but the structure of its neighborhood up to 3 hops away.
So the output is a set of informed per-atom embeddings.


encoder.py (MolecularEncoder) takes those per-atom embeddings and collapses them into a single fixed-size vector representing the entire molecule's state.
It does this via global mean pooling — literally averaging all atom embeddings — followed by a small MLP to project into your desired embedding dimension.
This is critical because your downstream models (time-lagged autoencoder, diffusion model) need a fixed-size input representing x(t),
and molecules can have varying numbers of atoms.

FOR ENCODER:

Node Features (atoms)
        ↓
MolecularGNN (message passing)
        ↓
Node embeddings
        ↓
global_mean_pool
        ↓
Graph embedding
        ↓
MLP projection
        ↓
Final embedding

These three fields are the core of how a Graph Neural Network (GNN) "sees" the molecule. Since you're looking at Peptide AV (Alanine-Valine), here is what those specific numbers represent:

1. x=torch.Size([29, 8]) (Node Features)
This tells the model what each atom is.

29: The number of atoms in the AV peptide.
8: The feature vector for each atom. In our processing.py script, these 8 slots are:
Indices 0, 1, 2: The [x, y, z] coordinates of the atom (so the model knows its location).
Indices 3, 4, 5, 6, 7: A one-hot encoding of the element. Only one of these will be "1" at a time:
[1,0,0,0,0] = Hydrogen (H)

[0,0,0,1,0] = Oxygen (O)
[0,0,0,0,1] = Sulfur (S)
2. edges=torch.Size([2, 80]) (Connectivity)
This tells the model how the atoms are connected (the chemical bonds).

2: It is an "edge list" where the first row is the "source" atom and the second row is the "destination" atom.
80: The number of directed edges. Since chemical bonds are undirected, every bond is listed twice (A→B and B→A). This means your AV peptide has 40 actual chemical bonds.
3. pos=torch.Size([29, 3]) (Geometric Positions)
This is a "pure" version of the atom coordinates.

29: The number of atoms.
3: The [x, y, z] coordinates in nanometers (nm).
Why is this separate from x? Many modern AI models (like Equivariant GNNs) need the positions kept separate from the "identity" features (x) so they can calculate distances and angles during the learning process.
In summary:

x = Identity + Position (What and Where)
edges = Topology (Who is connected to whom)
pos = Geometry (Exact coordinates for math operations)

the problem with the collapsing of the trajectory molecule is 
The atoms collapse because of a fundamental architectural limitation:

Your MLP receives a single 64-dim embedding per atom and must predict 300 frames × 3 coordinates = 900 values
from it. That embedding is computed from the first frame only — it has zero temporal information.
So the model has no idea how the molecule should move over time.

When faced with this impossible task, MSE loss causes the model to do the safest thing
mathematically: predict the average position across all 300 frames for every frame. 
The average of all positions is near the center of mass → all atoms get pulled toward the center → collapse.

MSE tells the model where atoms should be (the actual target coordinates)
Bond/Repulsion/Spread tell the model what not to do (don't compress, don't overlap, maintain structure)

MSE Loss (weight 1.0) — Matches predicted coordinates to ground-truth coordinates
Bond Length Loss (weight 5.0) — Keeps bonded atoms at the correct distance from each other
Repulsion Loss (weight 15.0) — Prevents non-bonded atoms from getting closer than 0.15 nm
Spread Loss (weight 10.0) — Prevents the whole molecule from shrinking by matching the radius of gyration to ground truth
Smoothness Loss (weight 0.5) — Prevents jerky/sudden jumps between consecutive frames


1. **MSE Loss**
   - In: `pred_traj [num_atoms, 300, 3]` + `target_traj [num_atoms, 300, 3]`
   - Out: `scalar`

2. **Bond Length Loss**
   - In: `pred_traj [num_atoms, 300, 3]` + `target_traj [num_atoms, 300, 3]` + `edge_index [2, num_edges]`
   - Out: `scalar`

3. **Repulsion Loss**
   - In: `pred_traj [num_atoms, 300, 3]` + `edge_index [2, num_edges]`
   - Out: `scalar`

4. **Spread Loss**
   - In: `pred_traj [num_atoms, 300, 3]` + `target_traj [num_atoms, 300, 3]`
   - Out: `scalar`

5. **Smoothness Loss**
   - In: `pred_traj [num_atoms, 300, 3]`
   - Out: `scalar`

All 5 scalars are then combined as:
```
total = 1.0*MSE + 5.0*Bond + 15.0*Repulsion + 10.0*Spread + 0.5*Smoothness
```
→ `scalar` → backpropagated

Yes, we are intentionally throwing away the global tumbling/drifting, because predicting it is virtually
 impossible and ruins the model.

Here is the fundamental reason why we must compare the prediction against the Kabsch-aligned Ground 
Truth (GT), rather than the raw unaligned GT:

1. The "Raw Input" contains random noise (Tumbling)
In a molecular dynamics simulation, the molecule is floating in a box. Over 300 frames, it does two 
things:

Internal Dynamics: It folds, bonds vibrate, and angles bend. (This is the physics we want the model to 
learn).
Rigid Body Motion: The entire molecule randomly tumbles, rotates, and drifts across the simulation box. 
(This is random noise).
2. Why we train on Kabsch-Aligned GT
If you force the model to predict the Raw Unaligned GT, it has to waste all of its brainpower trying to 
guess exactly how the molecule randomly tumbled and rotated in 3D space. Because tumbling is chaotic, 
the model fails to predict it, the MSE loss explodes, and the model completely ignores learning the 
internal dynamics (which is why your predictions were collapsing to a straight line or a single point 
earlier).

By applying Kabsch alignment to the "Actual Input" before it goes into the loss function, we 
permanently strip away the random tumbling. We are telling the model: "Forget about where the molecule 
drifted in the box. Just predict its internal shape."

3. Why we plot against Kabsch-Aligned GT
Because the model was trained exclusively to predict the Kabsch-aligned shape, its output will always 
be centered. If you plotted the model's prediction against the Raw Unaligned GT, the plot would look 
completely wrong—the raw GT would be drifting off-screen, while the prediction stays perfectly centered,
 even if their internal shapes matched perfectly!

Conclusion: The Kabsch-aligned GT is the true "ground truth" for the internal physics of the molecule. 
Comparing the predicted trajectory against the Kabsch-aligned GT is the standard, scientifically 
correct way to evaluate generative molecular models.

The reason your training loss is increasing linearly while the validation MSE stays flat is due to KL 
Annealing combined with the way your loss components are being tracked in the plot.

Here is exactly what is happening in your train_mlp.py script:

1. KL Annealing Weight (beta_kl)
In your training loop, you are using a technique called KL Annealing to slowly introduce the KL 
divergence penalty:

python
# KL annealing: β ramps from 0 → 1 over first 100 epochs
beta_kl = min(1.0, epoch / 100.0)
Because beta_kl linearly increases from 0.0 at epoch 1 to 1.0 at epoch 100, the contribution of the KL 
loss to your total training loss increases linearly during the first 100 epochs.

2. Free-Bits Clamp
Your KL divergence is computed using a "free bits" formulation:

python
loss_kl = kl_divergence_free_bits(mu, logvar, free_bits=0.5)
This guarantees that the loss_kl value is never lower than 0.5. As your beta_kl reaches 1.0 at epoch 100, 
the beta_kl * loss_kl term adds at least 0.5 to your total loss. You can see this exactly reflected in 
your graph: the training loss line ramps up and plateaus right around 0.5 after epoch 100.

3. Plotting Different Metrics
The blue line (Train Loss) is plotting the batch_loss, which is the grand total of everything: MSE + 
(beta_kl * KL) + DistMSE + VelMSE. Because the beta_kl * KL term is growing, the total line grows.
The orange line (Validation Loss) is only plotting loss_mse (coordinate MSE). It does not include the 
KL penalty.

Even though the model is trained to minimize the coordinate MSE, predicting individual frames independently 
can result in "jittery" or unsmooth trajectories. By explicitly punishing the model when the difference 
between consecutive frames doesn't match the difference in the ground truth, it forces the model to 
generate smooth, temporally consistent motion that physically makes sense. 

The Kabsch algorithm is a mathematical method used to optimally align two 3D molecular structures. 
It calculates the best possible way to translate (move) and rotate one set of coordinates so that it 
perfectly overlaps with a reference structure, minimizing the difference (RMSD) between them.

What is it doing here?
In a molecular dynamics (MD) simulation, a molecule moves in two distinct ways:

Internal Dynamics: The molecule folds, its bonds stretch, and angles bend. This is the actual physics you 
want the neural network to learn.
Rigid Body Motion: The entire molecule randomly drifts (translates) and tumbles (rotates) across the 3D 
simulation box. This is essentially chaotic noise.
The Problem: If you feed the raw (unaligned) ground truth into your model, the neural network is forced 
to predict both the folding and the random tumbling. Because tumbling is chaotic and unpredictable, the 
model gets confused, the loss function (MSE) explodes, and the model completely fails to learn the 
internal physics (often collapsing and just predicting a straight line).

The Solution (Kabsch Alignment): By applying the Kabsch algorithm to your ground truth data before 
training, you are completely removing the random translation and rotation. You are essentially "pinning" 
the molecule to the center of the screen so that the only thing moving is the internal folding and 
vibrating.

Why you use it:

For Training: You train the model on the Kabsch-aligned data so the neural network can say, "Okay, I don't 
need to guess where the molecule drifted in the box; I only have to learn how the bonds and angles change." 
This makes training stable.
For Visualization: Because the model was trained to predict a "pinned" molecule, it will output a pinned 
trajectory. Therefore, you must compare the predicted output against the Kabsch-aligned ground truth. If
 you compared the prediction against the raw unaligned ground truth, the raw molecule would be flying off 
 the screen while the prediction sits in the center, making it look like a failure

Your training dataset has 208 different dipeptides (AA, AC, AE, AF, ..., YY), and each one has its own 
unique dihedral trajectory. The diffusion model is trained on ALL of them together in a single model. 
So when you ask it to generate a trajectory, it needs to know "generate a trajectory for which peptide?"
— that's what the encoder does. It reads the molecular graph of a peptide (its atoms, bonds, element 
types) and compresses it into a 64-dimensional vector that captures the peptide's chemical identity and 
structural properties. This vector is fed into every layer of the denoiser so that the model can produce 
peptide-specific trajectories rather than some generic average across all 208 peptides. Without the 
encoder, you'd have a single model that generates the same trajectory regardless of whether you ask it 
about Alanine-Cysteine or Glycine-Glycine — which would be meaningless. The encoder is essentially the 
model's way of "seeing" the molecule before deciding how it should move. That said, you could technically
replace the GNN encoder with something simpler like a one-hot encoding of the peptide name (since you 
only have 208 discrete peptides), but the GNN approach is more general because it can handle unseen 
peptides at inference time by reading their graph structure directly, whereas a one-hot approach cannot 
generalize beyond the training set.
